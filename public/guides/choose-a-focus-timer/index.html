How to choose a focus timer app

Every timer has a start button and a countdown. What separates them is what happens at zero, what happens when you close the laptop, and what the app knows about your work when the day is over.

By Rajat Malik 17 November 2026

Start with what you need

A focus timer is a small piece of software with a small job. That is why the comparison tables all look the same after the third one: everything has a countdown, a dark mode and a break sound. The differences that matter are in the behaviour at the edges.

Before comparing anything, write down three things: the work you want to protect, the devices you use it on, and what you are willing to pay per year. The third number is usually zero for a timer, which rules out most of the category before you start.

Seven questions

1. Does it work when the network does not?

Try it with the Wi-Fi off. A timer that needs a connection to count down is a timer that will fail on the train, in a basement meeting room and during the one outage you remember. Offline support should be the default for something this small.

2. Where does the record of your work live?

A timer that records sessions is keeping a log of what you did all day. That log is worth protecting. Look for a local file, a browser storage you control, and a real export. If the answer is an account and a cloud, that is a choice — just make it knowingly. The guide on local-first software has the questions in full.

3. What happens at zero?

This is the question most reviews skip. A finished session should ring, show something, and offer a next step. If the alert is a sound you can miss and a notification that fades, the timer has done the easy half. If the alert cannot be answered without finding the window, it has failed at the moment it was needed.

4. Can you change the lengths?

Twenty-five and five are a fine default and a poor law. You should be able to set the focus length, the short break, the long break and the number of sessions before it. If the only choice is between themes, the app has decided something about your work that it has no business deciding.

5. Does it interrupt you, or ask you?

There is a difference between a notification that arrives and an app that brings itself to the front. The first is a reminder; the second is an interruption. For a timer that runs during presentations and calls, look for something that stays out of the way and can be answered from where it appears.

6. What does it cost over a year?

A subscription is not wrong, but for a timer it is worth doing the sum. A small monthly charge for a countdown is a charge you will still be paying in three years, for software whose core function has not changed. Free, one-off and subscription are all legitimate; the number per year is the thing to compare.

7. Can you leave?

Export your data before you commit, not after. A complete export, in a format something else can read, is the difference between using a tool and being kept by one. If you cannot find the export in the first five minutes, that is an answer.

Free, trial or subscription

Three models, and each has a different failure mode. A free app can disappear, or become a subscription later. A trial gives you a real look at the product and then asks you to decide before you have lived with it. A subscription guarantees nothing except the billing.

For a focus timer, the honest test is not the price list but the exit: can you take your data with you on the day you stop paying? If the answer is yes, the model is a fair trade. If it is no, the price is not the real cost.

Give it a week

Do not judge a timer by the first session. The first session is a novelty, and the second is a demo. What you want to know is whether it is still useful on Thursday, when the work is dull and the week has gone sideways.

Use it for one week on real work. At the end, answer three questions: did it help you start, did it help you stop, and did the record tell you anything you did not already know? Two yeses is a keeper. A timer that only helps you start is a kitchen timer with a subscription.

When a simple timer is enough

If your work is one kind of task, done in one place, and you do not want a record, a kitchen timer is genuinely sufficient. It has no account, no update and no opinion. The method does not need software; the software is for the parts a clock cannot do — the record, the task, the reminder and the review.

Buy the smallest thing that does the job you actually have. A timer with a planner, analytics and a journal is a poor choice if what you needed was a countdown, and a countdown is a poor choice if what you needed was to know where the month went.
