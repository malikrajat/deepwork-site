How long should a Pomodoro be?

Twenty-five minutes is the number everyone quotes, and it is a fine place to start. It is not the number that makes the method work — and treating it as sacred is how people conclude that the method does not work for them.

By Rajat Malik 17 November 2026

Where twenty-five comes from

The Pomodoro Technique was described by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, and the twenty-five-minute interval is the one he taught with. It has survived because it is a useful compromise: long enough to get somewhere, short enough that starting is not a decision, and short enough that a bad session costs less than half an hour.

That is all it is — a compromise that suits a lot of work. It is not a threshold below which focus does not happen, and there is no rule that says a session has to be twenty-five minutes for the method to count. If you work in longer arcs, a longer session is still a Pomodoro in the sense that matters: one task, a defined end, a break taken.

The shape, not the number

A Pomodoro is one task, one interval and one break. Change the interval and you still have the method. Drop the single task or the break and you have a countdown timer, which is a different and much less useful thing.

Three signals it does not fit

You do not need a system for choosing a length. You need to notice what happens at the edges of the session you already have.

Starting feels heavy every time

If the timer itself is the obstacle — you keep delaying the press, or you negotiate for “just five more minutes of email” — the session is probably too long for the state you are in. A shorter first session is not a lesser session; it is a way in. Many people do their first block of the day at fifteen minutes and the rest at twenty-five, and that is a reasonable arrangement.

You stop in the middle of a thought

The opposite problem. If the timer reliably lands while you are mid-sentence, mid-function or mid-decision, you spend the first minutes of every session finding your place again. A longer interval — thirty-five, forty, fifty — preserves the thread. The cost is that a difficult start is a bigger commitment, so this tends to suit work you already know how to begin.

The break is not enough, or is far too much

Five minutes is a break from the screen, not a break from the day. If you find yourself unable to return after five, the preceding session may have been too long rather than the break being too short. If five minutes feels like an interruption to something you were enjoying, that is a good sign: the session length is working, and the break is doing the job of stopping you before you hollow it out.

How to test a different length

Change one number and keep it for a week. A single day is too short to tell anything apart from the mood of the day, and changing the session, the break and the cycle count at once tells you only that something moved.

  1. Pick a length and a reason. “Forty minutes, because I keep stopping mid-thought” is testable. “Forty minutes, because I read that it is better” is not.
  2. Keep the break and the task rule fixed. One task per session, a break when it rings. Only the length changes.
  3. Write down one sentence at the end of each day. Did you start when you meant to, finish where you wanted to, and take the break? Three yes-or-no questions will tell you more than a feeling at the end of the week.
  4. Give it five working days. If two of the three answers are usually no, the length is wrong for this work — not for you as a person.

The numbers worth trying are the ones either side of what you already do: fifteen if twenty-five is heavy, forty if it is choppy. There is no need to hunt for an optimum. A length that makes starting easy and stopping clean is good enough.

Breaks are not optional

A session length is only half of an interval. The break is the other half, and it is the half people drop first when a day gets busy. That is backwards: the break is what makes the next session possible, and a day of unbroken sessions is a day of declining attention that still looks productive in a timer's history.

What counts as a break is not complicated. Stand up. Look at something further away than the screen. Drink water. Do not open the thing you were avoiding. A five-minute scroll through a feed is rest for the hands and not for the attention, which is why it so often turns a good session into a slow one.

The long break is the same idea at a larger scale. Fifteen minutes after four sessions is the convention; the point is that it is long enough to leave the desk and come back with the next arc in view. If you skip it, the fifth session is usually where the morning starts to cost more than it returns.

Different work, different lengths

One number for everything is convenient and slightly wrong. Writing, reviewing code, reading a contract and clearing an inbox are not the same kind of attention, and a length that suits one will be awkward for another. Two or three presets are enough:

  • Short, for starting and for chores. Fifteen minutes. Good for the task you have been avoiding, and for the admin that expands to fill whatever time it is given.
  • Standard, for ordinary work. Twenty-five minutes. The default for a reason: it fits most things and costs little when it goes wrong.
  • Long, for work with a thread. Forty to fifty minutes. Writing, debugging, design, anything where the useful state takes a while to build.

The mistake is not having presets; it is choosing one in the moment to avoid starting. Pick the length when you plan the day, not when the timer is already waiting.

Changing it in DeepWork

In DeepWork, the four numbers are settings rather than constants: focus length, short break, long break, and how many sessions come before a long one. The defaults are 25, 5, 15 and 4, so a fresh install behaves the way the method is usually taught, and nothing about the app assumes you will leave them there.

The break is part of the cycle rather than a separate timer you have to remember to start, which matters more than it sounds: a break that has to be initiated is a break that gets skipped. When a session finishes, the app asks what to do next, and stopping is one of the answers.

Try the length you think you need

Open the web app, set the session length in Settings, and run one block on real work. If the number is wrong, it will be obvious within a day — and changing it back is one field, not a new method.
