How to plan a day in focus blocks

A task list records what you owe. A plan decides what the day can actually hold. The difference is an estimate, a place in the day, and enough honesty to leave something out.

By Rajat Malik 17 November 2026

On this page

  2. Choose the work
  3. Estimate in sessions
  4. Place blocks and breaks

Why a list is not a plan

A task list is a container. It holds everything you might do, which is exactly what makes it a poor guide to a Tuesday: forty open items do not tell you what to do at nine in the morning, and they do not tell you what to stop doing at six. The list answers “what is outstanding?”. A plan answers “what fits?”.

Planning in focus blocks is a way of answering the second question with a unit you can actually feel. An hour is a unit of the clock; it can be spent in a meeting, on a call, or in six attempts to start the same document. A block is a unit of attention, and it has a shape: one task, a defined end, a break after it.

Choose the work

The first move is subtraction. Take the list and pick the few things the day is for. Not everything you would like to touch — the work that would make the day count even if the rest slipped.

Three groups are enough:

  • Must happen today. A deadline, a person waiting, a commitment you made. Usually one or two items.
  • Should happen this week. Real work with room to move. Pick at most two.
  • Would be nice. Everything else. This is not a to-do list for today; it is the pool you draw from when the first two groups are done.

The grouping matters because it gives you a way to stop. A day that ends with the first group done is a day that worked, even if the third group never moved.

Estimate in sessions

Estimating in minutes encourages a fiction: that nine hours of availability is nine hours of work. It is not. The useful estimate is how many sessions a piece of work will take, including the session that gets you back into it after an interruption.

Start with a number you can defend. “Draft the introduction” is one session. “Review the pull request” is one, maybe two if it is large. “Write the proposal” is not three sessions because you would like it to be; it is whatever it was last time. If you have never done it before, guess, then write the guess down. The guess is the data.

A useful rule of thumb

If you cannot name what “done” looks like at the end of the session, the task is too big to schedule. Split it until the end is visible. A session that ends in a decision or a paragraph is schedulable; one that ends in “more progress” is not.

Place blocks and breaks

Put the week's fixed commitments in first — the meetings, the calls, the school run — and then place focus blocks in what is left. Not the other way round: a plan that assumes the day is empty is a plan that collapses at the first calendar invitation.

Then apply three rules, in this order:

  1. Breaks go in before the fourth block. If the breaks are not on the plan, they will not happen, and the fourth block will be paid for by the third. A short break between sessions and a longer one after the cycle is the shape that keeps the afternoon usable.
  2. The hardest work goes where your attention is best. For most people that is earlier in the day, but not for everyone, and not on every day. Put the work that needs a clear head in the slot where you usually have one, and protect it.
  3. Leave a gap between blocks. Ten minutes is enough for the thing that overran, the question that arrived, and the walk back to the desk. A day built with zero slack is a day that is late by ten o'clock.

Leave the day room

A plan that accounts for every hour is not ambitious; it is a plan with no capacity for reality. Something will take longer, somebody will need you, and the tool you depend on will choose today to update. If the plan has no room for that, the only possible outcomes are overtime or a broken plan.

A workable default is to plan most of the available focus time and leave the rest unclaimed. The unclaimed part is not laziness; it is where the interruptions go, and it is the reason the rest of the plan survives them. When nothing interrupts you, the spare time is a gift you can spend on the third group from earlier.

Close the day

Five minutes at the end, before you close the laptop:

  • What actually happened, in sessions and in finished work?
  • What did not happen, and was that a planning error or a day error?
  • What is the first block tomorrow?

The third question is the one that earns its keep. A day that ends without a next first step starts with a negotiation, and the negotiation is usually lost to email. Writing the first block down moves the decision to a moment when you are not yet in the day.

When the day breaks

It will. The useful response is not to re-plan the whole day on the spot; that is how an hour disappears into a spreadsheet. Ask one question: what is the most valuable thing that still fits in the time left?

If the answer is “nothing that was on the plan”, that is information. Move the work to tomorrow, write down why, and use the remaining time on the small things that have been waiting. A plan is a tool for making a decision, not a contract with your past self. The guide on running a weekly review is where those daily notes become something useful.
