Method

The Pomodoro Technique, explained properly

It is a kitchen timer, five habits and a break you are actually supposed to take. Here is what the method asks of you, why the shape works, and where almost everybody quietly changes it into something that does not.

What a Pomodoro is

A Pomodoro is one timed interval of focused work followed by a short break. The word is Italian for tomato: Francesco Cirillo developed the technique in the late 1980s as a student, using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer to pace his studying, and the name stuck.

The conventional numbers are twenty-five minutes of work, five minutes of rest, and a longer break after four of them. They are the numbers the method is usually taught with, not a law of attention. Cirillo's own description treats the timer as a starting point that a person adjusts to the work in front of them.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A Pomodoro is a unit of attention, not a unit of the clock. The point is not to fill the day with twenty-five-minute slots; it is to give one piece of work a beginning, an end and a break that arrives before you have spent the good part of the morning.

The five steps

The method is small enough to write on a card, which is part of why it survives. In the order it is usually described:

  1. Choose one thing. Not a project and not a list — one piece of work you could plausibly move forward in a single sitting. “Draft the introduction” is a Pomodoro. “Write the report” is a week.
  2. Set the timer. Twenty-five minutes is the usual starting point. The act of setting it is the promise: for this interval, this is the work.
  3. Work only on that. When something else arrives — a message, a thought, a job for later — write it on a piece of paper and go back. The writing-down is what makes going back possible; trying to remember the interruption is how the session ends early.
  4. Stop when it rings. Take five minutes away from the screen. The break is part of the sitting, not a reward for finishing it, and skipping it is how the third hour of a morning becomes worse than the first.
  5. After four, stop for longer. Fifteen to thirty minutes, and away from the desk if the day allows it. That is the shape of a morning: a cycle of sittings, not a sprint with a countdown attached.

Nothing in those five steps requires software. A kitchen timer and a sheet of paper will do. What software adds is the record — which is useful, and which is also where the method starts to go wrong.

Why the shape works

There is no shortage of claims about what a timed interval does to a brain. This page will not make them: the method is a convention that many people find workable, and describing its mechanics is more honest than inventing a finding to sit underneath them.

The mechanics are worth stating plainly, because they explain what to keep:

  • A defined start removes a negotiation. “I will begin at 9:30 and work until 9:55” is easier to act on than “I need to get going”.
  • A defined end makes the work finite. A session you can see the end of is easier to start than a task with no boundary, which is why the timer helps most with the thing you have been avoiding.
  • One task per interval reduces switching. The cost of changing what you are doing is not the change itself but the time it takes to get back to where you were.
  • The break is scheduled, so it happens. A break that depends on remembering to take one is a break that gets postponed until it is no longer useful.
  • The interruption has somewhere to go. Writing it down is not administrative tidiness; it is the thing that lets you return to the session without holding the interruption in your head.

Where people go wrong

Most of the ways the technique stops working are not failures of discipline. They are the method being used for a job it was not designed to do.

Treating the count as a score

The moment a day is judged by how many Pomodoros it contained, the timer changes sides. It stops protecting the work and starts demanding it. A day with four real sessions and a difficult conversation can be a better day than one with eleven sessions and no finished thought. If the number is the goal, the work becomes a way of producing the number.

Skipping the break because the work is going well

This is the most common one, and it is understandable: the session is going well, so the break feels like an interruption to something that is finally working. But the break is not there to reward you for stopping. It is there so that the next session starts from somewhere other than the last one. Take it, especially when you do not want to.

Letting the timer outrank the room

A session is not a promise to ignore a person. If a colleague needs ten minutes, or the meeting starts early, the honest move is to stop the timer and pick the work up later. A method that has to be defended against your own day is a method that will be abandoned by Thursday.

Planning a day of sessions and nothing else

Sessions are not a plan. Deciding what the sessions are for is the part that decides whether the day was useful; the timer only decides how the time is spent. The guide on planning a day in focus blocks is the companion to this one for that reason.

What to change first

If the classic numbers do not fit, change one of them at a time and live with the change for a week. Changing all of them at once tells you that something is different, not what.

  • The session length. If starting feels heavy, shorten it. If you stop just as you reach the useful part, lengthen it. There is a guide on how long a Pomodoro should be.
  • The short break. Five minutes is enough to stand up, drink something and look at something further away than a monitor. It is not enough to answer a thread and come back.
  • The number before a long break. Four is conventional. If your work comes in longer arcs, three may suit you better; if it comes in short bursts, five is fine.

The thing to keep fixed while you experiment is the shape: one task, a defined end, a break that is taken, and a record you can look back at.

How DeepWork implements it

DeepWork is an independent implementation of the technique, not an official one: it is not affiliated with, endorsed by or licensed from the author of the method. What it does is take the five steps and make the parts a timer can hold do their job.

  • The defaults are the classic ones — 25 minutes of focus, 5 minutes of short break, 15 minutes of long break after four sessions — and all four are settings rather than constants.
  • A finished session rings, posts a system notification and keeps asking until it is answered. The alert itself carries Pause, Next, Resume and Hold up, so the answer does not require finding the window.
  • A session can be tied to one task, and the time is recorded against it. That record is what makes the review in the weekly review guide possible.
  • Focus mode hides the navigation and leaves the clock on its own. Every reminder keeps running, because a focus session is exactly when a reminder matters.
