Why reminders get ignored

Most reminders are not reminders. They are notifications: a rectangle that appears for four seconds, asks nothing, and is gone before you have decided whether it mattered. The things people actually forget need something with more patience than that.

By Rajat Malik 17 November 2026

On this page

  2. Three ways they fail
  3. What a reminder needs

A notification is not a reminder

A notification exists to tell you something happened. A reminder exists to make sure something gets done. The difference is what happens after the rectangle appears: a notification has finished its job once it is seen, while a reminder has not started until it is answered.

This is why so many reminders fail in the same quiet way. They are built as notifications — one fire, a short animation, an inbox entry — and then judged by whether the thing got done. When it does not, the conclusion is usually “I am bad at remembering”, which is not the problem. The problem is that the tool stopped asking after the first time.

Three ways they fail

It fires once, at the wrong moment

The reminder arrives while you are driving, in a meeting, or mid-sentence in the one piece of work you have managed to start all day. You cannot act on it, so you dismiss it — and dismissing it is the only way to make it stop. The reminder has now been answered, in the sense the software understands, without the thing being done.

The mute that stays on

Every reminder app has a silence control, and most of them implement it as a switch you have to remember to turn back on. You silence notifications for a meeting, forget, and spend three days wondering why nothing is arriving. The control that was supposed to protect your attention has quietly disabled the thing you rely on.

The app was not running

A reminder that lives in a process is only as reliable as the process. Close the laptop, restart, quit the app to save battery, and a scheduled timer can be gone. If the thing mattered — a dose, a train, a presentation — a reminder that ends when the window closes is not a reminder at all.

What a reminder needs

Four properties, and a reminder that is missing any one of them will eventually fail in the way described above:

  • Persistence. It survives a restart, a closed lid and a flat battery. It is stored, not merely scheduled in memory.
  • Escalation. If it is not answered, it comes back — sooner at first, then at a rhythm that is annoying enough to work and not so annoying that it gets muted. A single fire is not escalation.
  • An answer. Done, snooze for a stated time, or stop. Not a close cross, which is an answer to the notification rather than to the thing.
  • A place to land. The answer has to be possible where the reminder appears, on the card or on the notification, without navigating to a window that is behind the thing you are doing.

The interruption problem

There is a real tension here. A reminder that insists is a reminder that interrupts, and the moments when you most need to be reminded — a presentation, a class, a call — are exactly the moments when an app taking over the screen is unacceptable.

The resolution is not to soften the reminder. It is to separate asking from taking focus. A sticky notification and an in-app card can both wait for an answer without bringing a window to the front. The reminder stays insistent; the interruption stays small. A tool that brings itself forward in front of a room to ask whether you took a tablet has solved the wrong problem.

This is also why the notification and the card should carry the same answers. If the only place to press Done is behind the window that just covered your slides, the reminder has made itself the thing you have to deal with first.

Design for the answer

Once a reminder has an answer, the next question is what happens after a partial one. Snoozing is the most common answer in practice, and it should be honest: a fixed ten minutes, stated on the control, not an indefinite “later” that becomes never.

The same applies to a dismissed notification. Crossing it out is not the same as dealing with it, so a reminder that respects its own purpose comes back after a short interval. Three minutes is long enough not to feel like a fight and short enough that the thing is still in front of you. That one behaviour is the difference between a reminder that works and a reminder that trains you to ignore it.

What a standing reminder does

DeepWork has a feature built around this distinction, and it is honest about being unusual: a standing reminder is stored rather than scheduled, escalates rather than repeating on a fixed stopwatch, and keeps asking until it is answered.

  • Three rhythms, each a ladder of increasingly spaced repeats: Insistent at 3, 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes and then every 30; Steady at 10, 20, 30, 45 and 60 and then every 60; Gentle at 30, 45, 60, 90 and 120 and then every 120.
  • It survives a closed laptop and a restart, because it is a row in the local database rather than a timer in a process.
  • It asks one at a time and says how many are waiting behind it, so a stack of reminders is a queue rather than a pile of overlapping rectangles.
  • The only exits are Done, Snooze 10 min and Stop. There is no close cross and no timeout.
  • It posts a sticky desktop notification and an in-app card, and it never brings the window forward. A crossed notification returns after three minutes.

The design is not for every reminder. A reminder to water the plants does not need to escalate for an hour. It is for the things where being ignored is expensive: a dose, a train, a class, a presentation. Those are the ones a normal notification is worst at, which is why they are the ones worth building differently.
