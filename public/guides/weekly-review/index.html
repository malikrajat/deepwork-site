Review

A weekly review you can do in twenty minutes

The reviews that survive are the short ones. This one asks four questions, uses the record your timer has already kept, and ends with a single change rather than a new system.

By Rajat Malik 17 November 2026

On this page

  2. Before you start
  3. Read the week
  4. Name three truths
  5. Choose one change

Why reviews fail

Most weekly review systems are designed by people who enjoy designing systems. They have six sections, a backlog triage step, a monthly layer and a template in three colours. They are also thirty-five minutes long, which is why they survive two weeks.

A review has one job: turn last week into a decision about next week. Everything that does not serve that job is optional, and most of it is decoration. If the review is longer than the walk you would take instead, it will lose.

Before you start

Twenty minutes, one screen, and no new work. Open the record — the sessions, the tasks that were finished, the ones that were not — and close everything that can interrupt. Do not open the backlog. The point is to look backwards for fifteen minutes, not to start next week early.

Have somewhere to write three sentences. A notebook is fine. The journal in the app is fine. What matters is that the conclusion exists outside your head, because the version in your head will be revised by Wednesday.

Read the week

Start with what the record actually says, not with what you remember. Memory is kind to intentions and harsh on outcomes: you will remember the plan you made and the thing you failed to do, and forget the three days of work that went somewhere unglamorous but necessary.

Look for four things, in this order:

  1. Where the focused time went. Grouped by the work's title, not by task row. A recurring review that appears four times a week is a different fact from four unrelated tasks.
  2. What was planned and did not happen. Not to assign blame; to see whether it was moved, dropped, or quietly forgotten.
  3. What happened that was not planned. The interruptions are data. If the same kind of thing interrupts every week, it belongs in the plan rather than outside it.
  4. The shape of the week. Which days had sessions and which did not. A week with a hole every Wednesday is usually a meeting, not a motivation problem.

Name three truths

Three sentences, no more. The constraint is the point: it forces you to decide what actually stood out rather than listing everything that happened.

  • What worked. A kind of session, a time of day, a task that went well.
  • What got dropped. The work that was consistently postponed, and your best guess at why.
  • What kept interrupting. The recurring thing that was not on the plan.
A useful test

If a sentence could have been written before the week started, it is not a finding. It is a belief. “I was busy” is a belief. “Three of the five mornings went to the same review meeting” is a finding, and it suggests a change.

Choose one change

This is the part that makes the review worth doing, and the part almost every review skips. One change for next week, small enough that you could describe it in a sentence and notice on Friday whether it happened.

Good changes are specific and mostly boring:

  • “The first block on Tuesday and Thursday is writing, before email.”
  • “The weekly review moves to Thursday afternoon; Friday morning is for the report.”
  • “Three blocks, not five, and the spare time stays spare.”

Bad changes are aspirations: “focus more”, “stop procrastinating”, “be better at deep work”. They cannot fail, which means they cannot teach you anything.

Write next week

Before you close the review, write the three priorities for next week — not the whole plan, just the three things that would make it a good week. Then put the first block for Monday in the planner. Not the whole day; the first block.

The reason is the same one that makes the daily plan work: a decision made when you are not yet in the day is a decision the day has to argue with. A decision made at nine in the morning is argued away by the first message.

If the review has produced a change and a first block, it is done. Everything else is next week's problem, which is where it belongs.

What to ignore

Streaks. A streak rewards continuity and punishes the week you took a day off because you were ill. It is a measure of the app's persistence, not of your work.

Busyness. The number of sessions is not the value of the week. Ten sessions spent on the wrong thing are not better than four spent on the right one.

Other people's metrics. A public dashboard of someone else's focus hours is a different job, a different life and often a different definition of a session. Your record is useful because it is about your work.

The perfect week. The review is not looking for one. It is looking for the next honest step, which is usually smaller than the one you were planning to take.
